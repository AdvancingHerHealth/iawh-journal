From the IAWH Health Journal, Volume 8, Issue 10, October 2026.

When you sit on the examination table for an annual physical or a cardiac workup, your healthcare provider will often look up from a tablet and ask a standard screening question: “Do you feel safe in your home?”

It is a vital question, designed to catch the hidden signs of intimate partner violence. But for many women, the question calls up a specific picture: a volatile spouse, a controlling partner, or physical violence. If those elements aren’t present, a woman may quickly answer, “Yes.”

Your cardiovascular system, however, doesn’t read relationship labels. Your heart does not know the difference between the fear of an abusive partner and the constant, crushing anxiety of living with an aggressive, volatile teenager or adult child. To your nervous system, fear is fear, threat is threat, and a home without peace is a real hazard to your health.

This October, for Domestic Violence Awareness Month (DVAM), the Institute for the Advancement of Women’s Health (IAWH) is looking past the relationship labels to explore how chronic hostility in the home, whether from a partner or a child, can take a toll on a woman’s heart.

Leslie and Sharon: Two Realities, One Biological Response

To understand why the heart makes no distinction, consider Leslie and Sharon. They are composite portraits, not real patients, but their stories will be familiar to many women. Both arrive at the same DMV cardiology practice with worrying numbers: high blood pressure, a racing resting heart rate, and signs of inflammation.

Leslie

Leslie exercises, watches what she eats, and has never smoked. Yet her blood pressure keeps climbing. At home, she lives in the shadow of an unpredictable, controlling partner. There are no bruises, but she spends her evenings walking on eggshells, on high alert for the next emotional explosion.

Sharon

Sharon follows the same healthy habits. She, too, has never been hit by a spouse. But she lives in quiet fear of her high school-aged child, whose volatile, aggressive outbursts have left her afraid to parent, set limits, or speak up in her own home.

When asked, “Do you feel safe at home?” Leslie might hesitate, because she recognizes her partner’s behavior as abuse. Sharon will likely say “Yes,” because she sees her situation as a private parenting struggle or a family mental health crisis, not “abuse.”

Yet their bodies are responding to the same thing: ongoing threat.

The brain’s alarm system does not sort threats by their source. When a woman senses danger at home, her body releases stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline. Her heart rate rises, her blood vessels tighten, and her blood pressure climbs. That response is meant to protect us in a short emergency. But when the fear never lets up, the body stays on alert, and over time that strain can fuel inflammation, damage blood vessels, and raise the risk of heart disease, heart attack, and stroke.

The research on partner abuse is clear. A large study published in the Journal of the American Heart Association followed more than 18,000 women whose medical records showed domestic abuse. Compared with similar women, they had a 31% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, a 51% higher risk of type 2 diabetes, and a 44% higher risk of dying from any cause (1).

Women with recorded domestic abuse, compared with similar women. Chandan et al., Journal of the American Heart Association, 2020.

Researchers are now asking the same questions about stress that comes from children. A new study funded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute is examining whether teens’ stress “crosses over” to their parents’ heart health. Early pilot data found that on days when teens faced stressful events, their parents reported more negative mood and physical symptoms, and showed higher levels of cortisol (2). And child-to-parent aggression is more common than many realize: a 2025 review of studies from around the world estimated that about one in three young people studied had directed some form of violence toward a parent, most often psychological, such as threats, insults, and intimidation, rather than physical (3).

Redefining Safety: Beyond the Labels

Advancing women’s health means widening how both patients and providers think about stress at home. Coercive control, manipulation, and intimidation do not require physical contact to harm a woman’s health.

We must recognize that:

  • Living on edge is a health risk. Constant vigilance in your own home, no matter who is causing it, keeps the body’s stress response switched on, and chronic stress is linked to higher blood pressure and heart disease.
  • Shame keeps women silent. Women often hide the aggression of a teen or adult child out of deep shame, which can cut them off from both mental health support and heart care.
  • The coping trap compounds the risk. Living with fear often leads to lost sleep, depression, and leaning on comfort foods, salt, or alcohol to numb the pain, all of which add to heart disease risk.

Protecting the Hearts of Our Community

At IAWH, we believe you cannot fully care for a woman’s heart without considering the peace of the home in which it beats. If a provider asks whether you feel safe, know that your emotional peace, your daily anxiety, and your freedom from fear under your own roof are all part of the answer. It is okay to say, “Not always,” and to explain.

If you, a patient, or someone you love is living with partner abuse or a volatile family crisis, confidential help is available, including support, safety planning, and local advocates who can help restore peace to your home and your health.

Where to Find Help

If you are in immediate danger, call 911. All services below are free and confidential.

National (available anywhere in the U.S.)

  • National Domestic Violence Hotline: call 1-800-799-SAFE (7233), text START to 88788, or chat at thehotline.org. Available 24/7, and can connect you to local programs.
  • StrongHearts Native Helpline (for Native American and Alaska Native survivors): call or text 1-844-762-8483, or chat at strongheartshelpline.org. Available 24/7.
  • Love Is Respect (teens and young adults): call 1-866-331-9474 or text LOVEIS to 22522.
  • National Parent and Youth Helpline (for parents struggling with a child’s behavior or a family crisis): call, text, or chat 1-855-427-2736. Available 24/7 in English, Spanish, and many other languages.
  • NAMI HelpLine (when a family member’s behavior is tied to a mental health crisis): call 1-800-950-6264 or text “helpline” to 62640.
  • 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, 24/7.

Washington, DC

  • DC Victim Hotline: 1-844-4HELP-DC (1-844-443-5732), 24/7, or chat at dcvictim.org.
  • DC SAFE 24/7 Crisis Response Line: 1-800-407-5048. The District’s 24/7 domestic violence crisis intervention agency. dcsafe.org
  • My Sister’s Place: 24-hour hotline 202-529-5991. Emergency shelter, safety planning, and counseling. mysistersplacedc.org

Northern Virginia
