A Grateful Heart: The Diabetes–Heart Connection Every Woman Should Know This Thanksgiving

American Diabetes Month  |  November 2026

Thanksgiving is about the people around the table. This year, give your heart a little gratitude, too. Diabetes and heart disease are closely linked, and the connection hits women especially hard.

Why diabetes is a heart issue

November is American Diabetes Month, and this month also marks World Diabetes Day. When we think of diabetes, we often think of blood sugar. But the leading cause of death for people with diabetes is cardiovascular disease. People with diabetes are twice as likely to have a heart attack or stroke as people without it.

Over time, high blood sugar damages blood vessels and the nerves that control the heart. Diabetes also tends to travel with other heart risks, like high blood pressure and high cholesterol.

Why women should pay closer attention

Diabetes raises heart risk for everyone, but research shows women with diabetes face about twice the risk of heart disease that men with diabetes do. Women with diabetes are more likely to have heart attacks at a younger age, and those heart attacks are more often fatal.

Researchers point to several reasons. Many women already have high blood pressure or high cholesterol by the time they’re diagnosed. Women are less likely than men to have their blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol well controlled. And in the year after a diabetes diagnosis, women are less likely to be prescribed medicines that protect the heart.

The good news: type 2 diabetes can often be prevented or delayed, and every step you take for your blood sugar is also a step for your heart.

This year’s World Diabetes Day theme is Early Action on Diabetes. Nearly half of the more than half a billion people living with diabetes worldwide don’t know they have it. The earlier diabetes or prediabetes is caught, the more you can do to protect your heart, kidneys, eyes and nerves.

Enjoy the table: tips for a heart-smart Thanksgiving

Thanksgiving isn’t the time for guilt, and no single meal defines your health. These small, practical steps let you enjoy the day and still look out for your heart.

  1. Don’t skip meals to “save up.” Arriving starving makes blood sugar harder to manage. Eat a regular breakfast with some protein.
  2. Build your plate, then enjoy it. Start with vegetables and turkey, then add the favorites you look forward to all year. Savor them; just take one helping instead of three.
  3. Go easy on the salt. Gravy, stuffing, ham and casseroles can carry a lot of sodium, which raises blood pressure. Taste before you add more.
  4. Take a walk after dinner. Walking for as little as two to five minutes after a meal can help keep blood sugar from spiking. Make it a family tradition.

Enjoy your favorites. Balance, not guilt, is the goal.

  1. If you drink alcohol, eat with it. Alcohol can affect blood sugar and blood pressure. Alternate with water or sparkling water.
  2. Keep your medicines on schedule. If you’re traveling, pack enough for the whole trip, plus a little extra.
  3. Bring a dish you can feel good about. A roasted vegetable side or a fruit dessert gives you, and everyone else, another good choice.
  4. Rest and breathe. Holiday stress affects blood pressure and blood sugar, too. It’s okay to step away for a few quiet minutes.

Talk about “sugar” at the table. Having a parent, brother or sister with type 2 diabetes raises your risk. Our free Around the Table Family Health History Kit includes conversation starters and a chart to fill in with your family this Thanksgiving. Download it here.

Take early action

  • Know your numbers. Ask your provider about an A1C test, along with your blood pressure and cholesterol.
  • Find out if you have prediabetes. Take the CDC’s free, one-minute prediabetes risk test.
  • Ask about a lifestyle change program. The National Diabetes Prevention Program lowers the risk of developing type 2 diabetes by 58%, or 71% for people over 60.
  • If you have diabetes, ask about your heart. Talk with your provider about medicines that protect your heart, not just your blood sugar.
