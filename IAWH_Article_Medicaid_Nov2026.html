Medicaid Is Changing: How to Keep Your Coverage

Coverage Check, Part 2  |  Medicaid  |  November 2026

Open every letter from Medicaid.

Big changes to Medicaid start in the next few weeks. For many people, the biggest risk won’t be losing eligibility. It will be missing a letter, a deadline or a form.

The changes come from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1), the federal budget law signed on July 4, 2025. The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimates the law will reduce federal health spending by more than $1 trillion over ten years. It also projects about 10 million more people will be uninsured by 2034.

History shows why paying attention matters. When states rechecked Medicaid eligibility after the pandemic in 2023 and 2024, more than 25 million people lost coverage, and 69% of them were dropped for paperwork or procedural reasons, not because they were found ineligible. When Arkansas tried a Medicaid work requirement in 2018, about 18,000 people lost coverage within months, yet nearly all of those subject to the rule were already working or should have been exempt.

Staying on top of the changes could be the difference between keeping your coverage and losing it.

Here’s what’s changing, who it affects and what you can do now to stay covered.

States also face new limits on how they fund their share of Medicaid, starting in October 2027. That may squeeze state budgets in the years ahead.

Who: are you affected?

Most of the changes apply to the Medicaid expansion group: adults ages 19 to 64, not on Medicare, with incomes below 138% of the federal poverty level. That’s about $22,000 a year for one person or $45,000 for a family of four.

To keep coverage, adults in this group will need to show at least one of these each month:

  • 80 hours of work, job training, education or volunteering (or a mix)
  • Enrollment in school at least half time
  • Earnings equal to 80 hours at the federal minimum wage (about $580 a month)

Exemptions every woman should know

Many women qualify for an exemption and don’t know it. You do not have to meet the work requirement if you are:

  • Pregnant, or within 12 months after giving birth
  • A parent or caregiver of a child 13 or younger, or of a person with a disability
  • Living with a serious medical, mental health or substance use condition, or a disability
  • In treatment for substance use
  • A former foster youth age 25 or younger
  • A veteran with a 100% disability rating, or a member of a federally recognized tribe
  • Recently released from jail or prison (within 3 months), or recently in a hospital or nursing home
  • Living in an area with high unemployment or a declared disaster

Exact rules vary by state, so confirm with your state Medicaid agency.

What to do now

  1. Update your contact information. Make sure your state Medicaid agency has your current address, phone and email. Renewal notices that go to an old address are one of the most common reasons people lose coverage.
  2. Open every letter from Medicaid, and answer by the deadline. Starting this winter, expansion adults will hear from Medicaid twice a year instead of once.
  3. Know your exemption. If you’re pregnant, postpartum, caring for a young child or managing a serious health condition, say so on your application or renewal.
  4. Keep records. Save pay stubs, school enrollment letters, volunteer hours or a note from your doctor, in case your state asks for proof.
  5. Ask for help. Community health centers, legal aid offices and local social services departments can help you fill out forms.
  6. Have a backup plan. If you lose Medicaid, check HealthCare.gov or your state’s marketplace right away for other coverage. One catch: people dropped from Medicaid only for not meeting the work requirement can’t get Marketplace premium subsidies.

Help someone else. If you know a family member, a neighbor or a young adult on Medicaid, share this article with them. A two-minute address update could save their coverage.

In Virginia

  • When it starts: for people applying on or after Jan. 1, 2027, and for renewals due on or after March 31, 2027. Current members were sent a letter by the end of September 2026.
  • What counts: 80 hours a month of work, training, school or volunteering; half-time school; or earning at least $580 a month. Applicants must meet it in the month before applying.
  • Reporting in 2027: in most cases, what you report on your application or renewal will count as proof. Virginia will contact you if it needs more. Stricter rules begin in 2028.
  • Update your information: online at commonhelp.virginia.gov, by calling Cover Virginia at 855-242-8282 (TTY: 888-221-1590), or at your local Department of Social Services.

Outside Virginia? Forty-three states and D.C. must put work requirements in place. Timing and reporting rules differ. In D.C., contact the Department of Health Care Finance; in Maryland, contact Maryland Health Connection or your local Department of Social Services.
